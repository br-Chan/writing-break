I am Atlas of the Mountain.

When the Olympians sought to strike me down, I was swift to surrender – scrambling for sovereignty over a more putrid punishment. The Titanomachy, the primordial battle between the Titans and the Olympian gods, was ended, and the Olympian Gods meted their retribution as promised.

Cronus, our king, was brought before Zeus, his son and successor. Cronus was strung upon Zeus’s cosmic spindle, and he spun Cronus around and around and around – skin tearing, muscles bending, bones breaking, mind mangling. And so Cronus was spun into thread – a fine, golden thread that glistened in the darkness of a proto-world torn apart by the Titanomachy. The spindle was passed to the Moirai, the Fates. They wove a tapestry with the string that had been king, and henceforth the fabric of Time began.

Hyperion was brought before Poseidon. Hyperion was engulfed in the fires of the forge, and it roasted and rendered him into a dark, raging ball that was his soul. A shining, shimmering obsidian orb it was, and Hyperion’s screams of agony reduced to a whining hymn. So Poseidon struck him with his three-pronged tine, and Hyperion exploded into cascading oratorio. His angers, his musics, were stretched impossibly wide into the cosmos, and henceforth the symphony of Space began.

Coeus and Crius were brought before Demeter – as was her right for what they had done. She took the Sickle of Cronus and castrated them, then quartered their bodies, and then in her rage she swung the sickle back and forth, back and forth. So Coeus and Crius were sliced into a thousand trillion wriggling pieces; and still Demeter swung her sickle, until the titans two were a seething sea of energy and mass. She poured their atomised entrails into the expanding Hyperion. The pieces of Coeus and Crius, light and dark, surged across the infant Universe, and henceforth the song and dance of Matter began.

Iapetus, my father, was brought before Hades. Iapetus was heaved towards the upturned Helm of Hades, as the waters of the River Styx were poured into its darkness. Iapetus was pitched headfirst into the basin’s broth; and there his body boiled as Hades held him still. His soul dissolved into the acids of the Styx, leaving his body a small, crusted pit stuck fast to the Helm – but still my father survived in the waters. The living, milky spirits of Iapetus were poured into the halls of Time, Space and Matter. His soul was the milk that would nourish the Olympians to omniscience.

They say my father, in his dying – or living – breath, said his final words to the Olympians. But through the thrashing and bubbling as he drowned, no one now knew what it was he spake.

And, at last, the turn was mine. I had been crafty, I had thought, to have fought for the Titans but avoided the blame and the putrid punishment. I chose the path of dignity.

Zeus himself came to me. He spoke in sublime and benevolent voice, as an equal. He brought me to the rim of the infant Universe.

“Do you see the world we have wrought?” he said. “Do you see its chaos and infirmity? Do you see how your kin writhe?”

I see.

“Now, do you see what is above them all? Above Cronus and my thunderbolt spindle? Above Hyperion and his horrid verse? Above Coeus and Crius and their axial anguish? Above even your father? Do you see the Sky?”

I strained my eyes, and I saw it. A swirling space, pure, untouched by the frenzy of the Titans – a captivating vista, bearing down upon the burdens below. The two of us stood alone before its majesty.

“That is our celestial heaven, borne out of assymetry with the blood of the Universe.” Zeus, the new king, turned to me. “Before our war with the Titans, I promised that any god who sided against Cronus would retain their old titles under my rule. This I have done, and you are my last. Although you fought for Cronus at the onset of this war, your heart was changed and you saw the prudence in my sovereignty. For that I am glad, and I bestow upon you now a special honour. That is, if you are willing to to take it, with my sincerity as Zeus Areius, the atoning god, for the grievances we have caused.”

I bowed, but my lips were taut as I hid my secret joy. “I accept this gladly, and I am honoured to stand beside you in our sovereignty.”

Zeus did not reply.

He took me into the Universe, to a rocky planet besmirched by fire. “This planet is Mount Olympus. It is the anchor between the Olympian Sky and the Titanic Universe. Without it, the Sky would topple upon the Universe and both realms would be destroyed together – a mutual annihilation.”

I bent down and touched the dirt and rock of Olympus with my bare hands. It felt warm and soft… Like home.

“My father is here,” I said.

Zeus raised his arms. “Your father is everywhere. And so is the rest of your kin, serving the punishments they were given.”

A tremor hit the planet, and the land shook viciously.

“Mount Olympus cannot take the weight of the Sky alone,” Zeus declared. “It shall soon come crashing down upon your kin and end them all – and us. I entrust to you the most important honour in all creation: can you hold the Sky for us, for the mutual love of all that lives and is yet to live?”

I played the part of the submissive. I knelt before Zeus Areius and swore my unending fealty. I swore to atone for my transgressions against the Olympians and defend both their kin and my kin. I swore to hold the Sky as my sovereignty; as Atlas of the Mountain; as Atlas Telamon; as Atlas Enduring – until the thread of Cronus was spent.

For the first time, Zeus smiled. Then he laughed, a bitterness turned sweet and luscious.

“Farewell, Atlas Telamon.” And without another word he rose into his heaven.

“I shall be seeing you soon?” I asked, still kneeling. There was no response.

And then the Sky came crashing down upon my shoulders. I bore it with my back and pressed against it with my hands. Hard as rock it was, and yet soft as silk.

I tried to stand. The Sky did not budge. The Mountain was unyielding. I tried to ease the tension upon my spine, but my hold against the Sky waned and I felt it about to crush me. I tried even to move, but one shift of my pinky brought the whole world a-tremoring.

“Zeus,” I cried. “What have you done? What have I promised you?”

But I heard nothing – nothing but the echoed memory of his laugh, dark and evil like his father before him. There was a terrible, terrible silence in the Universe.

The burden grew. My muscles shivered and shook. It was heavy as the weight of the Olympians above me, heavy as the pains of my kin below me. Sweat dripped from my forehead and soaked into the ground at my feet, swirling with the concentrated spirits of my father inside.

I knelt there, waiting for someone to return. Zeus, magnanimous, would surely return with someone to bear the load with me? To take turns?

Perhaps he would return with his family, the new pantheon of gods to replace the Titans. They would celebrate here on Mount Olympus and give toast to their victory – our victory.

Perhaps he would bring my fellow Titans, my brethren! There were some who joined Zeus well before the fighting began: Oceanus and Tethys of the cosmic rivers; Helios and Selene of the celestial lights; Mnemosyne and Themis of the all-knowing laws.

Perhaps he would invite my brothers, wherever they had gone. Perhaps he would bring me my daughter, dearest Calypso.

I had begun to hear the Titans below me. I could hear Cronus on Zeus’s lightning bolt, a lamed, old madman as the Fates spun his destiny into fabric. I could hear Hyperion’s song, a pining tinnitus that longed for a time before cosmic webbing. I could hear Coeus and Crius, mindless, and I could bear witness to the miracles they wrought: planets and moons, comets and asteroids, stardust and supernovae – miracular, but mindless. I could hear my father in the recesses of my inner ear; whispering, whispering, whispering… But not to me. His whispering echoed into the abyss that was the Universe – Tartarus, I named it.

I could almost hear the Olympians up in the Sky that I held for them. I imagined them laughing at my expense as they partied in their palaces:

“What did you tell him, Zeus? That it was a special honour? And he believed you?”

“What was it that he called himself? Atlas Enduring?”

“Did he say he would stand beside us in our sovereignty? Beside us? The gall…”

Or maybe – and an icy cold filled my heart – they were not talking about me at all. Perhaps none of them were. Perhaps Zeus had not told the other Olympians what I had done, or believed in what I was doing for them. Perhaps Zeus had not returned because he had forgotten about me, and I was just another loose end to tie up like Cronus on his spindle.

No one was coming for me.

I called for help, but could not shout. I grew angry, but could not rage. I cried, but could not weep. Because shouting and raging and weeping would loosen my grip upon the Sky, and then it would all come crashing down. The Olympians in their high towers would fall, and the Titans in Tartarus would be crushed.

And would they not deserve it?

But what if Zeus came back? What if he came back to say it was all over – that it was a test? That I would finally join him among his ranks as a true Olympian? And what if I drop this, and it kills what little family I have left?

Even if they did not know it, I was saving them. I was saving them, for the rest of their lives and mine. And they would never know it.

The weight of the Sky was not what pained me, even as years turned into centuries into millennia. It was the way it hung in my heart – lowly, sunken, yet empty. It was the coldness of a ball of ice in my chest, chilling me and killing me.

It’s cold, and I’m shaking.

I could let go at any moment, and it would all be over. I could; I would; I should. But something stayed my hand.

Something moved on Mount Olympus. Carefully, I inched my eyes down to my feet. Far, far below, the planet had been transformed. My tears, my sweat, my spittle, had rained down in plumes across the planet. And under the planet’s surface – layered, latent – was a concentrated pocket of the essence of Iapetus, come here eons ago when Hades emptied his helm into these hallowed halls. And as the fluids of my torment and Iapetus’s torment combined – the joint pain of father and son – something became more than just essence.

Suddenly I could hear what Iapetus spake, billions of years ago as he drowned in the Helm of Hades. He had sworn vengeance against those that disbelieved in the worth of he and his sons.

The essence transformed into wriggling microscopic beings, and henceforth the tragedy of Life began.
